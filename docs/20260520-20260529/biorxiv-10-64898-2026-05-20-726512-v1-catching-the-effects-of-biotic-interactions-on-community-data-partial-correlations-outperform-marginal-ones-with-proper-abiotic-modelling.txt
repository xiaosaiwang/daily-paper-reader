Abstract 5

A major goal of community ecology lies in the deciphering of the processes underlying 6

species distribution. A widespread approach to this question is to identify patterns in species 7

community data and relate them to possible processes. Joint Species Distribution Models (JS-8

DMs) o ff er one way to do so through the infernece of association networks that describe patterns 9

of statistical correlations and dependencies between species, but it is unclear what processes can 10 

explain the presence of such correlations. While it has now been established that there is no 11 

equivalence between JSDM-inferred associations and biotic interactions, the later remain one 12 

possible explanation, among others, for the former. However, to our knowledge, there is no spe-13 

cific study of the statistical patterns induced by di ff erent types of interactions or of the conditions 14 

under which they may or may not appear as statistical correlations / dependencies in species 15 

communities. To explore these questions, we propose a "virtual ecologist" approach that consists 16 

in simulating community data based on abiotic and biotic processes with the VirtualCom model 17 

that emulates the e ff ects of environmental processes and of competition and facilitation interac-18 

tions. Then, we study to what extent JSDMs retrieve correlations between species that match the 19 

simulated interactions. We show that these interactions are better identified when using JSDMs 20 

that model partial correlations between species rather than marginal ones. We further demon-21 

strate how critical it is to correctly model abiotic e ff ects in order to identify biotic ones and that 22 

the "correct modelling" of these e ff ects depend on the type of interactions at stake. 23 

1. Introduction 24 

In ecology, community data describe the distribution of a set of species in a set of sites, 25 

either as presence-absence, count or biomass data. These distributions result from three types 26 

of mechanisms: dispersal, environmental filtering and biotic interactions (D’Amen et al., 2018; 27 

Guisan and Thuiller, 2005; Poggiato et al., 2021). Understanding these processes has been a 28 

long-term goal in ecology (Diamond, 1975; Connor and Simberlo ff , 1979) and can be critical to 29 

predict the responses of species communities to environmental stressors (Heinen et al., 2020). 30 

However, these processes cannot be directly observed in general. To decipher species distri-31 

bution data, a general methodology thus consists in identifying patterns in it (Gotelli and Graves, 32 

1996; Ulrich and Gotelli, 2010), with the idea that these patterns can be linked to underlying pro-33 

cesses or mechanisms. The complex relationship between processes, patterns and mechanisms 34

has been the subject of a number of analyses (Anand, 1994; McAllister, 1997; Connolly et al., 35 

2017). Anand (1994) proposes the following general definition: "patterns are what we perceive, 36 

processes describe how these patterns come about, and the mechanisms provide explanations as 37 

to why these patterns occur". In this work, we focus on patterns and processes as well as on the 38 

relationship between both concepts. Pattern detection is per se a di ffi cult question, among other 39 

reasons because the definition of a pattern is both investigator and scale dependent (McAllister, 40 

1997). The relationship between patterns and processes is also a complex one since di ff erent 41 

processes can have intermingled influences on species distributions and their e ff ects may also be 42 

scale-dependent (Götzenberger et al., 2012; Viana and Chase, 2019). 43 

Historically, statistical ecology aimed at identifying and describing biodiversity patterns 44 

(Gimenez et al., 2014; Poggiato et al., 2021), using null models for instance (Connor and Sim-45 

berlo ff , 1979; Ulrich and Gotelli, 2010). It has evolved towards more integrated modelling of 46 

ecological processes and the patterns they generate (Gimenez et al., 2014). Species Distribution 47 

Models (SDMS, Elith and Leathwick, 2009; Miller, 2010) and Joint Species Distribution Mod-48 

els (JSDMs, Pollock et al., 2014; Tikhonov et al., 2020; Chiquet et al., 2021)) are two recent 49 

classes of models that fit into this more recent framework. SDMs model each species’ distri-50 

bution data individually, relating it to abiotic covariates. Some of them additionally add other 51 

species as explanatory variable to model biotic interactions (Staniczenko et al., 2017; Poggiato 52 

et al., 2025) or integrate the modelling of dispersal e ff ects (Shipley et al., 2022). JSDMs are a 53 

more recent approach that simultaneously model all the species in the data and consider both the 54 

eff ects of environmental covariates and the residual correlations between species abundances, 55 

after accounting for the environmental covariates. They can be considered together as an associ-56 

ation network: the network’s nodes are the species and two species are linked by an edge if and 57 

only if their abundances are correlated given the e ff ects of the covariates. JSDMs are typically hi-58 

erarchical models with observations following a multivariate random distribution (Bernoulli for 59 

presence-absence data, Poisson log-normal for count data . . . ) conditionally on a latent Gaussian 60 

variable. The residual correlations can then be modelled by the variance-covariance (Pollock 61 

et al., 2014; Ovaskainen and Abrego, 2020) or precision (Chiquet et al., 2021) matrix of the 62 

latent variable. In the latent Gaussian layer, a partial residual correlation equal to 0 is equiv-63 

alent to the independence of the corresponding latent variables (Whittaker, 2009). The initial 64 

interpretation given to these models was that, in both SDMs and JSDMs, the e ff ects of covariates 65 

represented environmental filtering whereas, for JSDMs, residual correlations could be explained 66 

by biotic interactions, but also by missing predictors or model misspecifications (Warton et al., 67 

2015). It is now clear that there is no equivalence between biotic interactions and associations in 68 

a JSDM-inferred network (Blanchet et al., 2020; Poggiato et al., 2021). This does not however 69 

deny that biotic interactions have a possible role as one of the factors that can explain the pres-70 

ence of a residual correlations between two species (Gotelli et al., 2010). Thus, even in SDMs 71 

and JSDMs, a pattern-process-mechanism analysis still seems relevant. Indeed, while the pat-72 

terns are no longer what is directly observed in the species distribution data, they now emerge as 73 

the statistical e ff ects of abiotic covariates and the residual correlations identified by the models. 74 

Relating them to underlying processes still requires a careful analysis, when feasible (Poggiato 75 

et al., 2021). 76 

In this paper, we focus on the specific question of the interpretation of residual correlation 77 

patterns identified by JSDMs, in terms of processes and more precisely in terms of biotic in-78 

teractions. While no equivalence was ever established between biotic interactions and residual 79 

correlations, biotic interactions are often invoked as one of the main processes likely to explain 80 

residual correlations (Pollock et al., 2014; Warton et al., 2015; Tikhonov et al., 2020). Still, it 81

2. Materials and methods 108 

2.1. Generating species community data with the VirtualCom model. 109 

Several simulation models were considered to test the relationships between the processes 110 

induced by biotic interactions and the residual correlation patterns inferred by JSDMs. We con-111 

templated using a generalized Lotka-Volterra model for trophic relationships, inspired by Pog-112 

giato et al. (2025). However, these relationships are purely asymmetric, with opposite e ff ects for 113 

the species involved in an interaction (the prey’s presence favors its predator whereas the preda-114 

tor’s presence impairs its prey) so that it is unclear what pattern one could expect to find in terms 115 

of statistical dependence for a given trophic relationship. The EcoLottery model (Munoz et al., 116 

2018) was also considered but it lacks an explicit modelling of any type of biotic interactions. 117 

We finally decided to work with an adapted version of the VirtualCom model (Münkemüller and 118 

Gallien, 2015) as it directly models symmetric competition and facilitation interactions whose in-119 

terpretation in terms of patterns is more straightforward, with species in competition expected to 120 

be found together less often than others and the opposite patterns for species involved in facilita-121 

tion interactions. We briefly describe the model here but the reader should refer to Münkemüller 122 

and Gallien (2015) for an extensive description. We also detail how we modified the model to 123 

adapt it to the experiment we carried. 124

2.2. Tested JSDMs 171 

We compared the results obtained with three di ff erent JSDMs: the gllvm (Generalized Lin-172 

ear Latent Variable Model, Niku et al., 2019; Korhonen et al., 2025), Hmsc (Hierarchical Mod-173 

elling of Species Communities, Ovaskainen and Abrego, 2020) and PLN-network (Poisson Log-174 

Normal, Chiquet et al., 2019, 2021) models. All three approaches model a response variable 175 

(the presence / absence or abundance of all the species on each site) as a multivariate random 176 

vector following an appropriate distribution. The PLN-network model only allows the use of a 177 

Poisson log-normal distribution, suitable for count data, whereas the gllvm and Hmsc models 178 

additionally allow the use of other distributions such as Bernoulli for presence-absence data, but 179 

this study focuses on count data. In all three models, the response variable’s distribution param-180 

eters depend on covariates (usually environmental descriptors) and on a Gaussian latent variable 181 

whose variance-covariance matrix (for gllvm and Hmsc) or precision matrix (for PLN-network) 182 

represent inter-species correlations. 183 

The main di ff erence between the Hmsc and gllvm models on the one hand and the PLN-184 

network model on the other is that the first ones consider the association network given by a 185 

low-rank approximation of the latent variable variance-covariance matrix whereas the second 186 

one considers the network given by the precision matrix (inverse variance- covariance matrix). 187 

The variance-covariance matrix contains marginal correlations between the components of 188 

the latent variable whereas the precision matrix contains partial correlations between them. A 189 

partial correlations equal to 0 coincides with a conditional independence between the correspond-190 

ing components of the latent variable since it is Gaussian (Whittaker, 2009). This implies that a 191 

correlation found between two species A and B in the variance-covariance matrix could be ex-192 

plained by the statistical e ff ect of a third species C from the data whereas in the precision matrix, 193 

these e ff ects are corrected for. 194 

In terms of semantics, throughout the paper, residual associations refer to correlations ob-195 

tained after correcting for the e ff ects of covariates (the environmental descriptor and /or a species-196 

specific intercept) be they marginal (from the variance-covariance matrix) or partial (from the 197 

precision matrix) ; marginal associations refer to the residual associations obtained with the 198 

variance-covariance matrix based network, after correcting for the e ff ects of covariates but not 199 

for those of the other species ; whereas partial associations refer to the residual associations 200 

obtained with the precision matrix based network, after correcting for the e ff ects of covariates 201 

and of the other species. 202 

Moreover, the three models resort to di ff erent inference methods. The Hmsc inference is 203 

based on a Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo approach, the gllvm model uses a Laplace 204 

approximation whereas the PLN-network model uses variational inference. 205 

For each model, we tested three approaches to the modelling of the environment, with this 206 

environment being described by a single value along a numerical gradient in the VirtualCom 207 

model. In the first option, we did not include the environmental covariate at all and only used 208 

a species-specific intercept as covariate. In the second one, we directly included the continuous 209 

environmental descriptor as a covariate. Finally, in the third one, we treated it as a categorical 210 

covariate. To this end, we simply divided the environmental gradient into nbin categories and 211

2.3. Random networks 221 

For reference, we also compared each ground-truth network to a series of random networks 222 

with increasing edge density. For each simulation, we started from an empty network and added 223 

one edge at a time until the network was full. Each edge’s type ("facilitation" or "competition") 224 

was drawn at random, using the probability given by the facilitation / competition ratio used in 225 

the simulation. 226 

2.4. Simulation study 227 

2.4.1. Questions to explore 228 

The main goal of this work was to test the strength of the relationship between the patterns 229 

that emerge as residual (marginal or partial) correlations in JSDMs and competition or facilita-230 

tion interactions. To this end, we tested to what extent interactions that are explicitly defined as 231 

competition / facilitation interactions in VirtualCom simulations (the processes) could be identi-232 

fied in the residual correlations (the patterns) inferred by the di ff erent JSDMs. We further tested 233 

how this ability depends on the intensity and density of the interactions. Real species distribu-234 

tion data are the result of a sampling process and this process may miss a number of species in 235 

a community, owing to sampling errors / limits, or to the low abundance of some species for in-236 

stance (Blasco-Moreno et al., 2019). Therefore, we also wanted to test if the retrieving of species 237 

interactions by JSDMs is robust to the removal of some of the species, either picked as those 238 

with the lowest abundances or at random. Finally, we considered the case of asymmetric inter-239 

actions, either competition / neutral or facilitation / neutral (one species’ presence is negatively 240 

or positively a ff ected by the other, but the converse is not true). 241

2.4.3. Metrics 270 

Before considering JSDMs, one can first wonder whether the patterns in the simulated data 271 

correspond to what one would intuitively expect given the way VirtualCom was designed: given 272 

two species with close niche optima, they are expected to be found together less (resp. more) 273 

than the other pairs species with similar niche proximity if there is a competition (resp. facili-274 

tation) interaction between them. To test this correspondence Si-moussi et al. (2020) proposed 275 

a Relative Abundance Index (RAI). For two species j, k, when n sites are considered along the 276 

environmental gradient, we define: 277 

RAI jk = avg {δi, j|1 ≤ i ≤ n y i j > 0, yik > 0}

with δi, j = yi j − ¯y j,

with yi j the abundance of species j at site i and ¯ y j its average abundance over all the sites. This 278 

asymmetric index is the empirical transcription of the idea that if j is in competition with k, then 279 

when both species are present j’s abundance should be lower than it is on average . However, 280 

it fails to account for the situation in which the competition is so strong that the two species are 281 

never found together. It is essentially an empirical test to ensure that the simulation "does what 282 

is says" and is correctly parameterized. 283 

The RAIs are measured separately for four categories of species pairs: competing species, 284 

facilitating species, species with no interactions but close niches (below Dthreshold so that they 285 

should still often occur together) and species with no interactions and distant niches. 286 

For the proper JSDM testing, we consider that there is a correspondence between a biotic 287 

interaction in the simulation and a correlation pattern in the JSDM if two competing species are 288 

identified as having a negative residual correlation between their abundances for competition and 289 

a positive one for facilitation. More precisely, in this framework, a "true positive" corresponds 290 

to a pair of species with a competition (resp. facilitation) interaction in the simulation set-up 291 

and a negative (resp. positive) residual (marginal or partial, depending on the model) correlation 292 

inferred by the JSDM. 293

(resp. negative) association or no association at all in the JSDM-inferred network. Therefore, 306 

the higher the AUC is, the stronger the correspondence between the simulation processes (biotic 307 

interactions) and the JSDM patterns (statistical associations). 308 

2.4.4. Simulations parameters 309 

The number of species is set at p = 20, the number of sites at n = 50, the carrying capacity 310 

at K = 100. By default, the competition and facilitation intensities are fixed at 2 and −2 respec-311 

tively, and their densities at 0 .2 and 0 .1 respectively. The sites are distributed over a gradient 312 

from 0 to 100, the species niche breadth is fixed at 2 and the distance threshold below which 313 

interacting pairs of species are drawn is Dthreshold = 10. The relative weights of the di ff erent 314 

eff ects, βenv , β comp and βabun are all fixed at 1. 315 

The number of "years" over which the simulation is run is a compromise between the sim-316 

ulation’s computational time-cost and our need to see the species distributions converge for our 317 

analyses to be based on the e ff ects of the simulated ecological processes and not on the random 318 

initialization. To test the convergence, we measure the mean di ff erence in abundance for each 319 

species over all the sites between two years. We empirically see that running it over 10 "years" 320 

is long enough (see Figure 2), resulting in an average simulation time of 16 seconds with the 321 

default parameters. 322 

To explore the results’ robustness when changing the interactions’ intensity and density, we 323 

test di ff erent intensities (0 .1, 0.5, 2, 5) and di ff erent densities (0 .05 , 0.1, 0.2, 0.5) for facilitation 324 

and competition. The "species ratios" tested to see if the models correctly retrieve the interac-325 

tions between a fraction of the species when given access to the distribution data of these species 326 

only are 0 .75 and 0 .5 (that is we either kept only 75% or 50% of the species, either the most 327 

abundant ones or species drawn randomly). To test the robustness of the results to asymmetric 328 

interactions, we finally re-run the simulations with the default parameters but asymmetric in-329

Each configuration was repeated 20 times. 332

The RAIs (Figure 3) show that the simulated data is consistent with what can be expected 338 

from the mechanisms at stake: species with competition interactions are found together less often 339 

than species with no interaction, whereas species with facilitation interactions are found together 340 

more often. As could also be expected, the more intense the interactions get, the stronger these 341 

eff ects are. The RAIs also underline a possible source of confusion for the JSDMs: species with 342 

close niches also tend to be found together more often than species with distant niches, even 343 

when they have no interaction. Niche closeness could therefore be misinterpreted as facilitation 344 

if the e ff ects of the environmental covariate (responsible for species with close niches being 345 

found together more often than the others) are not adequately separated from the e ff ects of biotic 346 

interactions in the model. 347 

For competition, the best AUCs, around 0 .75 are obtained with the PLN-network model 348 

when the environmental covariate is treated categorically, with an ideal number of bins of 10 or 349 

15 in general (Figures 4, 5). These results seem slighlty improved when the competition and 350 

facilitation intensities increase (Figure 4). Increasing the density of competition interactions also 351 

appear to increase the AUCs, up to a certain point (beyond a density of 0 .1 it does not change 352 

much). The facilitation intensity has no significant impact on the competition AUCs. In all 353 

the tested configuration, the gllvm and Hmsc AUCs stay around 0.5, that is roughly the values 354 

obtained for the random networks (Figures 4, 5). For the PLN-network model, not accounting 355 

for the covariate e ff ect makes the competition AUC even worse than what is obtained at random 356 

and accounting for it as a continuous covariate or with a division in categories that are not small 357 

enough also significantly alter the AUC. For the gllvm and Hmsc models, conversely, the method 358 

used to include the e ff ects of environmental covariate does not appear to influence the results. 359 

Giving the PLN-network model access to only a subset of the species (Figures 6, 7) appear to 360 

decrease the AUCs but this e ff ect is stronger when the subset is selected at random rather than 361 

based on the species’ abundance. The other models’ results do not appear to be impacted by this 362 

subsampling. 363 

For facilitation, the gllvm and Hmsc AUCs also appear similar to what is obtained at ran-364 

dom (Figures 8, 9). For the PLN-network model, the results appear completely reversed from 365 

the competition ones (Figures 8, 9): the model with no environmental e ff ect obtains the higher 366 

AUCs and the AUCs decrease as the number of categories the environment is divided in in-367 

creases. Increasing the facilitation intensity relatively to the competition one appears to improve 368 

the PLN-network AUC (Figure 8). Symetrically to the competition results, changing the den-369 

sity of facilitation interactions from 0 .05 to 0 .1 significantly improves the AUCs but increasing it 370

obtained with the gllvm and Hmsc models, but all the AUCs obtained with the PLN-network 373

We have introduced a simulation experiment that falls into the "virtual ecologist" approach 377 

(Zurell et al., 2010) to question the relationship between statistical patterns and the processes 378 

defined by biotic interactions in the context of JSDMs. In this section, we start by analysing the 379 

correspondences between statistical correlations and competitive interactions for the di ff erent 380 

JSDMs, depending on the type of correlations they consider, and the robustness of these results 381 

to a number of changes in the parameters. Then, we question the di ff erence between the results 382 

obtained for facilitation and competition. This leads us to the issue of correctly accounting for 383 

biotic e ff ects and how the simulations results underline this point. Finally, we discuss the limits 384 

of this experiment and the perspectives it opens. 385 

4.1. Competitive interactions can partially be retrieved in partial correlations at a level that is 386 

robust to a range of perturbations in the simulations. 387 

As patterns directly observable in the data (that is, with no refined statistical transforma-388 

tion) the relative abundance indices confirm the consistency between the interactions modeled 389 

by VirtualCom and the species distribution data. The idea that residual (marginal or partial) cor-390 

relations as inferred by JSDMs may relate to biotic interactions is based on the same intuition 391 

as RAIs: species in competition (resp. facilitation) co-occurr less (resp. more) than species with 392 

no interactions (Morales-Castilla et al., 2015; Freilich et al., 2018) (although there is no clear 393 

proof that co-occurrences can inform biotic interaction networks in general, see Freilich et al. 394 

(2018); Poggiato et al. (2021)). Thus, we in the simple, artificial VirtualCom framework, we 395 

expect JSDM-inferred association networks to match, at least partially, the simulated biotic in-396 

teractions, with negative residual correlations observed where competition occurs and positive 397 

ones for facilitation. 398 

The results show a relatively good match between competition and negative associations for 399 

the Poisson log-normal models that consider the "environmental" covariate categorically ("PLN 400 

- categorical" on Figures 4 to 7) with AUCs consistently around 0 .75. This implies that the 401 

competitive interactions are partially but not fully retrieved in the network of partial correlations 402 

obtained after accounting for the environmental e ff ect. Conversely, the gllvm and Hmsc models 403 

fail to retrieve these interactions as negative marginal correlations, with no significant di ff erence 404 

between the results obtained with the di ff erent modelling of the environmental covariate. These 405 

results appear robust to changes in the interactions intensities and densities, and even to the 406 

subsampling of the most abundant species. Subsampling the species however appears to reduce 407 

the AUCs. In the VirtualCom framework, each species’ influence on the simulation process is 408 

proportional to its abundance on each site so that the most abundant species should be the most 409 

influential ones. This is probably why the removal of the less abundant species does not a ff ect 410 

the results as much as removing them at random does. 411 

We also ran the simulations with asymmetric interactions (see Appendix A). Given the sim-412 

ulation framework we could only test two types of asymmetry, "facilitation / neutral" (e.g. com-413 

mensalism) and "competition / neutral" (e.g. ammensalism), but not interactions with opposite 414 

eff ects ("competition / facilitation", like parasitism) since it is not clear whether one would expect 415 

to retrieve a statistical association in this case, and which one it would be. Surprisingly, the PLN-416 

network results with a categorical modelling of the covariate appear robust to the interactions 417 

becoming asymmetric. Indeed, the symmetry constraints of partial-correlations-based JSDMs 418 

is often mentioned as a strong limit to JSDMs’ ability to retrieve biotic interactions (Dormann 419 

et al., 2018). While our results do not contradict this point in general, given the limited type of 420

asymmetric interactions we consider, we show that in the stereotypical VirtualCom framework, 421 

asymmetric competition interactions are still partially identified as negative partial correlations, 422 

with no striking decrease in the AUCs compared to the symmetric configuration. JSDMs how-423 

ever, do not allow one to know whether a statistical correlation may correspond to a symmetric 424 

or asymmetric interaction and, in the latter case, if both species are negatively impacted but with 425 

di ff erent intensities or if only one of them is impacted by the other’s presence. 426 

These results show that, in this framework, competitive interactions are hard to retrieve 427 

through marginal correlations, probably because the direct and indirect e ff ects of species on 428 

each other cannot be disentangled. Resorting to a network of partial correlations appear more 429 

effi cient provided the environmental e ff ects are correctly accounted for. Yet, even in this rather 430 

simple framework (there are only two types of interactions, a limited number of species and a 431 

one-dimensional environmental gradient) the AUCs hardly reach values beyond 0 .8. This con-432 

firms the di ffi culties inherent to the task of relating (statistical) patterns to biotic mechanisms: 433 

while competition appears as a legitimate hypothesis to explain a negative residual association, 434 

it requires further experimenting to be confirmed. 435 

Overall the results also underline how critical it is to account for abiotic e ff ects here. This can 436 

be explained by the opposite e ff ects that abiotic e ff ects and competition have in this framework: 437 

species can only be in competition if their niches are close enough so that they still have more 438 

chances of being found together than species with distant niches that would not be in competition. 439 

This is probably what explains the low AUCs for the PLN model with no covariate. 440 

For facilitation, however, abiotic and biotic filters have synergestic e ff ects and this could 441 

explain the di ff erence in the results we obtained. 442 

4.2. The appropriate approaches and interpretations depend on the type of interactions consid-443 

ered. 444 

For facilitation, the RAIs still show that the general patterns seem consistent with what one 445 

expect the simulation to do. The AUC results are the same as for competition for the gllvm and 446 

Hmsc model but are strikingly reversed for the PLN-network model. The major di ff erence with 447 

competition is that, this time, the abiotic and biotic filters have synergestic e ff ects, as the RAIs 448 

clearly show: two species involved in facilitation have two reasons to be found together - their 449 

niches are close and they favor each other’s presence. 450 

Therefore, it is likely that when accounting for the environmental e ff ect facilitation is "ab-451 

sorbed", in the abiotic filters through the "mean e ff ects" of the underlying generalized linear 452 

model. However, when the covariate is not included, both the abiotic and biotic filters appear 453 

in the positive residual correlations, with the strongest positive correlations observed for species 454 

with facilitation interactions. As a side e ff ect, it is likely that species with very close niches are 455 

also more likely to be mistakenly identified as being involved in facilitation interaction. 456 

These results underline that a JSDM’s ability to disentangle di ff erent mechanisms (biotic, 457 

abiotic...) through the statistical patterns it unveils strongly depend on the joint dynamics of 458 

these mechanisms and more specifically, whether their e ff ects are synergestic or antagonistic, an 459 

idea that Poggiato et al. (2021) also underlined with a di ff erent approach. 460 

4.3. The importance of correctly accounting for abiotic e ff ects. 461 

These di ff erences between competition and facilitation appear related to the di ff erence be-462 

tween the PLN-network with a continuous and a categorical environmental covariate results 463 

in the sense that it raises questions about the appropriate way to deal with abiotic covariates. 464

We have discussed why the gllvm and Hmsc networks did not match the simulations’ interac-465 

tions networks and why accounting for covariates when it comes to facilitation appear altogether 466 

counterproductive in this simulation framework and now focus on competition and on the PLN-467 

network model. 468 

When accounting for covariates, several approaches can be used. The most natural one, 469 

used in several JSDMs, including the PLN-network model, is to resort to a generalized linear 470 

model (GLM) and directly plug the covariates in it, with no or little transformation. However, 471 

in this simulation framework, this does not appear as the most relevant approach. Indeed the 472 

(virtual) environmental covariate covers a large gradient and the environmental filter is based 473 

on the distance between a species’ niche and the local environment. A GLM with no covariate 474 

transformation then does not appear appropriate as it cannot model the proximity between an 475 

environment and a niche but only general trends. To get closer to the niche modelling, we 476 

consider a basic idea: discretising the covariate. This cuts the gradient into nbin bins, allowing a 477 

GLM to infer one coe ffi cient per pair ( species , bin ) that can be low when the bin is far from 478 

the species niche and gets higher as the bin gets closer to the niche. 479 

This simple approach proved fruitful: the PLN-network model perform much better with a 480 

categorical covariate than with a continuous one allowing the model to reach mean AUCs around, 481 

or higher than, 0.75 when using a continuous covariate leaves it stuck around 0.6 (Figure 4). It 482 

also appears that the division of the gradient into categorical covariate requires to be optimized 483 

as using too few bin or too many bins reduces the AUCs. This actually questions one of the limits 484 

of this approach: nbin is a hyper-parameter, we have not identified a clear, e ffi cient way of fixing 485 

it. 486 

4.4. Limits and perspectives 487 

Although this experiment shows that some JSDMs can partially retrieve symmetric and asym-488 

metric competitive interactions provided they properly account for abiotic e ff ects, there are limits 489 

to these results. First, although the VirtualCom model is designed to simulate realistic ecological 490 

processes (Münkemüller and Gallien, 2015), it remains a stereotypical framework, with interac-491 

tions modelled in accordance with the same intuition as the one that underpins the relationship 492 

between biotic interactions and JSDM-inferred biotic interactions. 493 

Moreover, it is unclear to what extent the species distribution data that VirtualCom outputs 494 

can be compared to any real species distribution data set. When using a JSDM for a specific 495 

species distribution data analysis, it could be useful to re-use this framework and adapt the sim-496 

ulation parameters to obtain configurations that are closer to the considered dataset. 497 

While our results show a certain ability of JSDMs to disentangle antagonistic e ff ects and 498 

thereby identify competitive interactions, the results are di ff erent when considering facilitation 499 

interactions and synergestic e ff ects of the di ff erent filters. Other interactions could lead to even 500 

more complex e ff ects and statistical patterns, such as parasitism or trophic interactions. 501 

Finally, the approaches we explore here could be further refined when it comes to the han-502 

dling of abiotic e ff ects. While the simple discretisation of the covariate proves to work well, 503 

other more robust approaches could be explored for this issue, especially when wide environ-504 

mental gradients are considered, for instance using splines bases. Integrating splines in one of 505 

the models would however require significant modifications of the inference method. 506

5. Conclusion 507 

We have introduced an original virtual framework to test the relationship between statistical 508 

patterns identified by JSDMs and the processes induced by two symmetric biotic interactions, 509 

competition and facilitation. 510 

We have shown that competition can be partially retrieved by the PLN-network model but is 511 

not identified by the other models. This is likely explained by the PLN-network model using a 512 

network of partial correlations instead of marginal ones as gllvm and Hmsc do. Still, even in this 513 

stereotypical framework the AUCs do not exceed 0 .8. Competition is not perfectly retrieved in 514 

partial correlations but appears as a legitimate hypothesis to explain the presence of a negative 515 

association in partial association network. This does not identify the PLN-network model as a 516 

"best JSDM" since models that were not tested here, or refinements of the other tested models 517 

may work too, but rather shows as a proof of concept that JSDMs have the ability to (partially) 518 

retrieve such interactions in this stylised context. The very di ff erent results obtained for facili-519 

tation with the highest AUCs obtained with a model that did not account for the environmental 520 

covariates and low AUCs for the PLN-network model with covariates, prove that a model’s abil-521 

ity to identify statistical patterns that can help disentangle multiple processes depend on the joint 522 

eff ects of these processes and, specifically, whether they are synergestic or antagonistic. Overall, 523 

all of these results were only weakly impacted by the changes of parameters (interactions inten-524 

sities and densities) with the exception of the species subsampling (especially if done at random) 525 

that significantly reduces the AUCs for both competition and facilitation. 526 

These results show that there is a partial correspondence between the processes induced by 527 

facilitation and competition as they are understood and modelled ecologically, and the statistical 528 

patterns inferred by JSDMs so that the latter can be used to decipher the first. We have also 529 

underlined that this correspondence changes depending on the models and types of interactions 530 

considered. These relationships should be analysed with care, paying attention to the inclusion 531 

and modelling of abiotic filters as well as to the potential influence of missing species. 532
