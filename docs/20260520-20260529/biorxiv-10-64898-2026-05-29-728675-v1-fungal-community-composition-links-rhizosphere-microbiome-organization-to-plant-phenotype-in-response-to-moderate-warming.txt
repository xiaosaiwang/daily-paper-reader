ABSTRACT 29 

Background: Global warming increasingly challenges plant performance and ecosystem 30 

function, and plant responses to elevated temperature are shaped not only by intrinsic plasticity, 31 

but also by interactions with rhizosphere microbial communities. However, it remains unclear 32 

how warming-induced microbiome reorganization relates to plant performance, whether 33 

microbiome community composition predicts plant phenotypic responses to elevated 34 

temperature better than simple host-microbiome origin matching, and which bacterial or fungal 35 

community features contribute most strongly to this prediction. 36 

Results: We implemented a full factorial design manipulating plant genotype (P), microbial 37 

inoculum (M), and temperature (T) using natural Arabidopsis thaliana ecotypes and their 38 

corresponding rhizosphere microbiomes from contrasting climatic regions. By integrating 39 

high-throughput plant phenotyping, microbial community profiling, microbiome-phenotype 40 

coupling analyses, and predictive modeling, we found that elevated temperature induced a 41 

coherent thermomorphogenic shift in plant architecture, while plant phenotypic variation 42 

remained determined primarily by genotype over the 14-day vegetative growth period covered 43 

in this study. Overall, cold-origin genotypes showed larger temperature-induced trait shifts than 44 

warm-origin genotypes. Rhizosphere microbial communities were structured predominantly 45 

by inoculum origin, but were also affected by warming. Evidence for a increased 46 

thermomorphogenic growth when genotypes were combined with inoculum from their home 47 

site was limited. Instead, microbiome-phenotype associations were better captured by 48 

community composition than by matching status, with fungal community variation showing 49 

stronger and more consistent associations with plant phenotype than bacterial variation and 50 

providing more robust predictions of plant phenotypes across models. 51 

Conclusions: Warming reorganized plant-rhizosphere systems within persistent host- and 52 

inoculum-associated baselines. Plant phenotypic variation during vegetative development 53

BACKGROUND 62 

Rising global temperatures are reshaping ecological systems from organismal physiology to 63 

community organization [1]. Climate projections indicate that plants will increasingly 64 

experience thermal regimes exceeding historical baselines [2], with consequences for species 65 

distributions, biodiversity patterns, and agricultural productivity. Plant responses to warming, 66 

however, are embedded in ecological networks rather than expressed in isolation. In terrestrial 67 

ecosystems, these networks include plant-associated microbial communities that interact 68 

closely with roots and influence plant nutrition, development, and stress tolerance [3–5]. 69 

Microbial communities are themselves shaped by plant hosts, environmental conditions, and 70 

interactions among microorganisms. Understanding how these biotic and abiotic forces jointly 71 

influence microbiome-mediated plant performance is therefore essential for predicting 72 

ecological responses to climate warming and for maintaining ecosystem functioning under 73 

rising temperatures [3]. 74 

Plants are sessile organisms that must continuously integrate environmental signals into 75 

developmental programs to sustain growth and reproduction. In the model plant Arabidopsis 76 

thaliana , moderately elevated ambient temperatures (approximately 26-28 °C) induce 77 

coordinated morphological changes, including petiole elongation, hyponastic growth (upward 78

termed thermomorphogenesis, reflect a reallocation of growth that helps maintain 80

thermomorphogenesis has been intensively studied, plant responses to warming are not 82 

determined by plant physiology alone. Root-associated microbial communities can influence 83 

plant growth through nutrient mobilization, hormone modulation, and protection against 84 

environmental stress [10–13]. Temperature-driven changes in rhizosphere microbial 85 

communities may therefore interact with plant thermomorphogenic responses and shape plant 86 

architecture under elevated temperature [3,14]. 87 

The rhizosphere microbiome is structured by multiple interacting drivers, including plant 88 

genotype, microbial source environment, and abiotic conditions such as temperature [15–18]. 89 

In many systems, soil origin explains a substantial proportion of microbial community variation, 90 

establishing a baseline upon which host filtering and environmental perturbations act [17,19]. 91 

Large-scale surveys of A. thaliana across Europe have provided important insight into these 92 

assembly processes. Continental-scale analyses revealed strong geographic structuring of soil 93 

microbial communities, reflecting environmental heterogeneity across sites, whereas root-94 

associated bacterial assemblages showed partial convergence across locations, suggesting that 95 

a limited set of phylogenetically diverse bacteria repeatedly colonizes plant roots despite 96 

differences in surrounding soils [17]. Reciprocal transplant experiments between 97 

geographically distant A. thaliana populations further showed that plant adaptation across 98 

climatic gradients is driven primarily by local environmental conditions, while soil microbial 99 

communities contribute comparatively little to the magnitude of adaptive differentiation [16]. 100 

These studies highlight the joint roles of host genotype, environmental context, and 101 

microbiome assembly in shaping plant-microbiome systems. 102

Temperature is a fundamental regulator of microbial metabolism and growth [16–18]. 103 

Warming experiments across ecosystems have documented shifts in microbial community 104 

composition and associated ecosystem processes under elevated temperature [20–22]. 105 

Microbial taxa often occupy distinct climatic niches, and warming can therefore drive taxon-106 

specific changes in microbial richness and phylogenetic structure [23]. Plant-associated fungal 107 

communities frequently show stronger compositional responses to elevated temperature than 108 

bacterial communities, pointing to potential kingdom-level differences in sensitivity to thermal 109 

perturbation [24,25]. Beyond taxonomic composition, warming may also alter the ecological 110 

organization of microbial communities by changing interaction patterns and overall community 111 

structure. Such changes can influence the balance between cooperative and competitive 112 

associations and may affect microbiome stability. Because plant-associated microbiomes 113 

regulate processes central to plant performance, including nutrient acquisition, hormone 114 

signaling, and stress tolerance, warming-induced reorganization of plant-microbe interactions 115 

has the potential to affect both plant phenotype and ecosystem functioning. 116 

Despite growing recognition that plant-associated microbiomes influence plant 117 

performance, the mechanisms linking warming-induced microbiome reorganization to plant 118 

phenotypic responses remain poorly resolved. It remains unclear whether elevated temperature 119 

overrides host- and soil-origin effects on rhizosphere community composition or instead adds 120 

temperature-dependent shifts while preserving differences associated with plant genotype and 121 

soil inoculum origin. It is also unclear whether plant responses to warming are equally strongly 122 

associated with bacterial and fungal community composition. 123 

To address these questions, we implemented a fully factorial design manipulating plant 124 

genotype (P), microbial inoculum (M), and temperature (T) using natural Arabidopsis thaliana 125 

ecotypes and their corresponding rhizosphere microbiomes from contrasting climatic regions. 126 

We used 16 °C as a cool reference condition and 28 °C as a warm treatment, thereby 127

Experimental treatments and growth conditions 181 

To investigate the three-way interaction among plant genotype (P), microbial inoculum (M), 182 

and temperature (T), a full factorial design (P × M × T) was established. Seven A. thaliana 183 

genotypes were used: three from Spain, three from Sweden, and the reference genotype Col-0 184 

(1001g ID: 6909). These were grown in seven soil treatments: three carrying Spanish microbial 185 

inocula, three carrying Swedish microbial inocula, and one sterilized-soil control, under 16 °C 186 

and 28 °C conditions. The experimental design is shown in Fig. 1 . Climatic background data 187 

for the collection sites were obtained from CHELSA-derived environmental layers compiled 188 

in AraCLIM V2.0 within Arabidopsis CLIMtools [27]. We used the CHELSA spring mean 189 

temperature (CHELSA Tmean spring) and spring maximum temperature (CHELSA Tmax 190 

spring) variables to describe long-term temperature conditions at accession origins, because 191 

spring better approximates the main vegetative growth window of A. thaliana in its natural 192 

habitats. Although mean seasonal maximum temperatures at the collection sites remained 193 

below the 28°C setpoint ( Table S1 ), the 16-28 °C temperature regime was chosen to a) 194 

maintain comparability with the Arabidopsis thermomorphogenesis literature, and to b) ensure 195 

a significant extent of thermomorphogenic plant growth behaviour. As such, 28 °C represent 196 

an upper-end warming scenario rather than a simulation of average spring temperatures in the 197 

field. 198

climatic origin as warm (Spain) or cold (Sweden) where appropriate. For the primary home– 200 

away analysis, only the exact pairing between a plant accession and its native soil inoculum 201 

was classified as home. All other non-native combinations, including pairings within the same 202

Plant phenotypic trait recording and data analysis 225

performed using LemnaTec software (LemnaGrid, LemnaExperiment, Analysis Executor4, 229

traits. Day 0 refers to the onset of the temperature treatment, and the plant trait analyses 231

treatment. Petiole length and petiole angle were additionally quantified from endpoint images 233 

using ImageJ. Fresh weight was also recorded at harvest. A total of 11 plant traits characterizing 234

Rhizosphere microbiome data processing and community analyses 272 

Raw sequencing reads were processed using QIIME2 (v2025.7; [33]). Demultiplexed paired-273 

end reads were merged using FLASH2 and denoised with DADA2 to generate amplicon 274 

sequence variants (ASVs; [34,35]). Chimeric sequences were removed using the uchime-275 

denovo approach implemented in QIIME2. Taxonomic assignment was performed against 276 

SILVA v138.1 for bacterial 16S rRNA sequences and UNITE v9.0 for fungal ITS2 sequences. 277 

ASVs classified as chloroplast, mitochondria, or unassigned at the kingdom level were 278 

removed. To reduce sparsity, ASVs present in fewer than 10% of samples or with total 279 

abundance <20 reads across all samples were excluded. Unless otherwise stated, downstream 280 

analyses were performed on relative abundance tables normalized to unit total abundance. 281 

Bacterial and fungal communities were profiled with different amplicon markers and analyzed 282 

separately. We therefore limited comparisons between kingdoms to broad patterns such as 283

Soil property associations with microbial community composition 286 

Associations between soil physicochemical properties and major microbial phyla were 287 

assessed using Mantel tests. Relationships between microbial community composition and soil 288 

physicochemical variables were further examined using constrained ordination, with canonical 289 

correspondence analysis (CCA) applied to bacterial communities and redundancy analysis 290 

(RDA) applied to fungal communities. Mantel tests [36], CCA [37], and RDA [38] followed 291 

the linear constrained ordination framework implemented in the vegan package (v2.7-3) [39] 292 

in R. 293 

Alpha and beta diversity analyses 294 

Alpha diversity was calculated from ASV tables rarefied to 1,000 reads per sample using the 295 

Shannon index, Chao1, and observed richness. Beta diversity was quantified using Bray-Curtis 296 

dissimilarity based on relative abundance data, and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was 297 

used for ordination. Community composition was tested by PERMANOVA with 999 298 

permutations, using temperature, microbial inoculum, plant genotype, and their interactions as 299 

explanatory terms. Multivariate dispersion was assessed separately by testing distances to 300 

group centroids with 999 permutations. Analyses were performed on the full dataset and within 301 

each temperature treatment (16 °C and 28 °C) in R using the vegan package (v2.7-3) [39–41]. 302

Microbial network construction and topological analysis 311 

Microbial co-occurrence network analysis was used to examine whether elevated temperature 312 

was associated with changes in potential microbial association patterns in the rhizosphere. 313 

Microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed separately for the 16°C and 28°C 314 

treatments using the Molecular Ecological Network Analysis Pipeline (MENAP; Institute for 315 

Environmental Genomics, University of Oklahoma) [42]. Prior to network construction, taxa 316 

detected in fewer than 2% of samples were removed to reduce sparsity. Pairwise associations 317 

between relative abundances of different Phyla were calculated using Spearman rank 318 

correlations, and adjacency matrices were defined using the random matrix theory (RMT)-319 

based threshold implemented in MENAP. Only correlations exceeding the RMT-derived cutoff 320 

were retained for network construction. Network topological properties, including node degree, 321 

clustering coefficient, modularity, transitivity, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector 322 

centralization, were calculated within MENAP to assess temperature-associated changes in 323 

network connectivity and compartmentalization. Modules were identified by modularity 324 

optimization. For downstream analyses, module eigengenes were defined as the first principal 325 

component of the standardized relative abundances of taxa within each module. Co-occurrence 326 

networks represent statistical associations; network differences were therefore interpreted as 327 

changes in potential microbial association structure rather than direct ecological interactions. 328

Associations between accession-level growth index and microbiome compositional change (Δβ) 338

most pronounced for plant area, for which fungal predictors increased adjusted R² and reduced 604 

AIC, while bacterial predictors did not improve the model ( Table S16 ). 605 

Cross-validation analyses further showed that adding fungal community composition axes 606 

produced reproducible predictive gains across traits ( Fig. 7d-f ), with consistent increases in 607 

cross-validated R² relative to the baseline model and to models adding bacterial community 608 

composition axes. Observed-versus-predicted relationships showed good correspondence 609 

between fitted and measured values (Fig. S26 ), supporting the robustness of fungal 610 

composition-derived predictors. In full models including both fungal and bacterial axes ( Table 611 

S16 ), fungal PCoA1 remained a significant predictor for all three traits, whereas bacterial 612 

PCoA1 was not significant. Semi-partial R² analyses and standardized regression coefficients 613 

(Fig. S27; Table S18 ) likewise indicated larger unique and standardized effects for fungal than 614 

bacterial composition-derived predictors. 615

DISCUSSION 617 

In reciprocal plant-soil systems, an important unresolved question is whether warming disrupts 618 

historically structured plant-microbiome associations or instead reshapes them within existing 619 

ecological baselines [3,22,51]. In this study, warming did not simply override pre-existing 620 

plant–soil–microbiome relationships, but reshaped them in ways that remained dependent on 621 

the biological structure already present in the system. Temperature effects were clearly 622 

detectable across both plant phenotypes and rhizosphere communities, yet these effects were 623 

expressed through persistent host- and soil-associated baselines rather than as uniform 624 

responses. Our results also provided only limited support for simple origin-based matching of 625 

plant genotypes and inocula as a general explanation for plant performance. Instead, 626 

microbiome composition, particularly fungal community composition, was more closely 627 

associated with endpoint vegetative performance than host-microbiome origin matching alone. 628 

Therefore, plant responses to warming in this system were best understood as emerging from 629 

thermal reorganization acting on pre-existing biological structure, rather than from temperature 630 

effects in isolation. 631 

632 

Temperature strongly reprograms plant phenotype while reorganizing rhizosphere 633 

communities within established ecological scaffolds 634 

Elevated temperature was a major driver of both plant phenotype and rhizosphere community 635 

structure, but in neither case did it erase the pre-existing biological context in which these 636 

responses were expressed. On the plant side, warming induced a coherent shift in architecture, 637 

including petiole elongation and coordinated changes in rosette-related traits, consistent with 638 

well-described thermomorphogenic responses in A. thaliana [6,47]. Plant genotype explained 639 

a larger share of endpoint vegetative trait variation than microbial inoculum. At 28°C, cold-640 

origin accessions tended to produce larger final rosettes than warm-origin accessions, 641

indicating that warm-origin accessions did not show a general endpoint phenotype advantage 642 

under elevated temperature ( Figs. 3 and S14 ). Under our experimental conditions, these 643 

patterns provide limited support for a simple local climatic adaptation interpretation 644 

[16,17,52,53]. Instead, the results show strong genotype-specific thermal responses, but 645 

provide limited support for an adaptation to local climate [47,54]. One caveat is of course 646 

implemented by the experimental design with an unusually high ambient temperature at both 647 

the northern and southern sites during the monitored developmental stages. However, all 648 

phenotypes reported here were measured within 14 days of the temperature shift, a standard 649 

window for Arabidopsis thermomorphogenesis assays, under conditions broadly applied in the 650 

thermomorphogenesis literature. These inferences are largely limited to vegetative 651 

developmental stages under constant long-day photoperiod and two setpoint temperatures; later 652 

developmental stages when microbial effects may potentially become more pronounced and 653 

fluctuating regimes were not assessed [55,56]. 654 

A comparable pattern was evident in the rhizosphere microbiome. Although temperature 655 

significantly shifted the composition of both bacterial and fungal communities, these shifts 656 

were secondary to the strong structuring effect of the origin of the microbial inoculum ( Figs. 657 

4, S16 and S17 ). In both kingdoms, soil-derived inoculum explained most of the variation in 658 

community composition, whereas temperature imposed reproducible directional changes 659 

within this inoculum-defined framework. This pattern is consistent with previous work 660 

showing that environmental filtering can reshape plant-associated microbiomes without fully 661 

overriding the influence of source community composition, edaphic history, or geographically 662 

structured microbial pools [16,17,57,58]. Warming therefore acted less as a replacement force 663 

than as a modifier of an already structured assembly background. 664 

Plant and microbial responses to warming were parallel in one important respect: both 665 

were strongly influenced by pre-existing variation. Plant phenotypic responses depended on 666

with additional host effects that were particularly evident in fungi ( Figs. 3 and 4 ). Elevated 668 

temperature did not impose a uniform response across plant-soil systems, but instead interacted 669 

with historically and biologically structured baselines to produce context-dependent outcomes. 670 

This view is consistent with broader work showing that responses to climate-related change 671 

are filtered through pre-existing host and community structure rather than determined by 672 

temperature alone [17,58,59]. It also helps frame the downstream links between microbiome 673 

structure and plant performance, which in this system emerged against persistent host and soil 674 

constraints. 675 

676 

Warming selected recurrent microbial lineages and reorganized rhizosphere community 677 

structure 678 

The microbial response to warming was clearly directional rather than random. Across 679 

sampling sites, the abundance of several bacterial and fungal families repeatedly differed 680 

between temperature treatments, often with consistent response directions ( Fig. S20 ). Such 681 

deterministic filtering under elevated temperature, indicate that warming did not simply 682 

increase compositional noise but repeatedly favored or disfavored particular lineages across 683 

distinct plant-inoculum contexts [60–63]. Thermal change may therefore constrain rhizosphere 684 

assembly by selecting a narrower subset of community configurations able to persist under 685 

warmer conditions [42,64]. 686 

This selective restructuring was also evident at the level of community organization. Under 687 

28 °C, the rhizosphere network showed higher connectivity, a greater proportion of bacteria-688 

fungi links, and lower clustering, transitivity, and modularity than at 16 °C ( Figs. 5 and S21 ). 689 

Although co-occurrence networks do not identify the causal ecological interactions, these shifts 690 

indicate that warming was associated with a less compartmentalized and more reconfigured 691

favored, but also how bacterial and fungal groups were arranged relative to one another within 693 

the rhizosphere. In biological terms, this points to reorganization at the level of community 694 

architecture, with potential consequences for how different microbial groups respond jointly to 695 

the rhizosphere environment under warming. 696 

Notably, these taxonomic and structural changes were not accompanied by strong 697 

divergence in predicted functional profiles ( Fig. S22 ). Warming therefore appeared to reshape 698 

community membership and organization more strongly than broad predicted functional 699 

categories, a pattern consistent with the partial decoupling of taxonomic and functional 700 

responses often observed in microbial communities [63,65,66]. Given the limitations of 701 

PICRUSt2- and FUNGuild-based inference, this result should be interpreted cautiously [67,68]. 702 

Repeated lineage-level turnover, network rewiring, and relatively stable predicted functional 703 

structure point instead to structural reassembly within a constrained functional space rather 704 

than wholesale functional replacement [63,65]. This matters for the interpretation of later 705 

microbiome-phenotype relationships, because plant performance under warming was 706 

associated with a microbiome that had already been selectively filtered and reorganized, rather 707 

than simply displaced. 708

Host-microbiome matching was limited and temperature-dependent 710 

Previous work has suggested that long-term associations between plants and their local soil 711 

microbiota can generate home-field advantage or contribute to microbe-mediated local 712 

adaptation, potentially improving host performance or microbiome persistence in native 713 

combinations [16,69,70]. Empirical support for such effects, however, has often proved to be 714 

context-dependent rather than universal [19,71,72]. In our system, home-away effects on 715 

rhizosphere turnover were limited, being detectable only in bacterial communities at 16 °C and 716

signal ( Fig. S23 ). Rather than supporting a general home-associated stabilization of the 718 

microbiome, these results indicate that matching effects on community composition were weak, 719 

temperature-dependent, and kingdom-specific. Consistent with this, host-microbiome origin 720 

matching did not improve the prediction of endpoint plant phenotypes in our models. Under 721 

lower temperature, historically associated host-microbial inoculum combinations may retain 722 

enough ecological continuity for differences in bacterial turnover to emerge, whereas stronger 723 

thermal filtering under warming may reduce the expression of these finer-scale association 724 

[16,17]. 725 

726 

Microbiome-phenotype relationships were better captured by community structure than 727 

by matching status 728 

If identity matching was not a general explanation for rhizosphere stability, it was also unlikely 729 

to be the main route through which the microbiome influenced plant phenotype. Instead, our 730 

results indicate that microbiome-phenotype coupling was captured more effectively by 731 

variation in community structure than by the simple distinction between home and away 732 

combinations. This was already apparent in the Mantel analyses, where fungal community 733 

dissimilarity showed stronger and more consistent associations with plant phenotypic 734 

dissimilarity than bacterial community dissimilarity under both matching contexts ( Fig. 6c-e ). 735 

In this system, the effect of the microbiome on plant phenotype therefore depended less on 736 

whether hosts and microbiota shared a common origin and more on how rhizosphere 737 

communities were compositionally organized [58,73–75]. 738 

Across analyses, fungal communities showed stronger plant-related structuring during 739 

assembly ( Fig. 4a, c ), stronger community-level coupling with plant phenotypic variation in 740 

the Mantel tests ( Fig. 6c-e ), and more consistent predictive gains when fungal composition-741

were “more important,” but that fungal community organization may have integrated host-745

traits such as nutrient acquisition, carbon exchange, and close physical association with roots, 748

composition (Fig. S24 ), suggesting that phenotype-relevant microbiome effects were 754

fungal community composition rather than from individual network modules ( Figs. 7 and S24 ). 757 

This shift suggests that warming altered not only microbiome composition, but also the 758 

organizational level at which microbiome variation affected plant phenotype and growth. 759 

Rather than uniformly amplifying or suppressing microbiome effects, warming appears to have 760 

shifted the relative importance of local interaction features of the microbiome toward emergent 761 

properties of the fungal community as a whole [42,76]. Therefore, the key result is not simple 762 

origin matching, but rather that plant phenotypes under warming were more closely associated 763 

with the overall organization of the rhizosphere microbiome, especially its fungal component 764

Implications for plant-microbiome coupling under warming 767 

These patterns suggest that, under warming, rhizosphere organization may provide a more 768 

useful framework than host-microbiome origin matching for understanding how microbiomes 769 

relate to plant performance. In particular, the consistent signal associated with fungal 770 

community structure points to a level of organization that may be informative for future efforts 771 

to identify microbiome features linked to plant function under changing temperature conditions 772 

[69,78]. Rather than focusing only on whether native host-microbiome combinations are 773 

maintained, an important next step will be to determine which community states remain 774 

associated with plant performance as environments shift. 775 

Future work should test these associations more directly and in ways relevant to 776 

microbiome-based improvement of plant performance. In particular, cross-inoculation 777 

experiments with reduced-complexity or synthetic communities could help determine whether 778 

the fungal composition-derived signal identified here reflects a causal contribution to plant 779 

growth under warming or a broader rhizosphere state associated with improved performance 780 

[80,81]. Direct functional measurements and validation under field conditions or fluctuating 781 

thermal regimes will also be needed to assess how stable and transferable these structure-based 782 

relationships are outside controlled environments. 783

CONCLUSIONS 785 

Under our controlled growth chamber conditions, elevated temperature was associated with 786 

reproducible shifts in plant architecture and rhizosphere community structure within host- and 787 

inoculum-determined baselines. Home-away matching explained little of the observed 788 

phenotype variation, whereas broader microbiome organization, particularly fungal community 789 

composition, carried more consistent phenotype-related signal under warming. Our results 790 

therefore indicate that plant responses to elevated temperature were linked less to origin-based 791
