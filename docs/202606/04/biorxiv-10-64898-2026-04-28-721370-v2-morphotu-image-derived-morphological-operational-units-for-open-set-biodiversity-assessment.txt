Abstract 21 

The absence of a scalable system for organizing the vast majority of unidentified 22 

species is a central obstacle in biodiversity science. Molecular methods can generate 23 

OTUs without species names but require sequencing infrastructure and often remain 24 

difficult to link to observable morphology, whereas most computer-vision methods 25 

still rely on closed-set species labels. These limitations hamper biodiversity 26 

quantification under the open, incomplete conditions that characterize real ecosystems. 27 

Here, we introduce morphOTUs, a general image-based framework that constructs 28 

operational units of biodiversity directly from phenotypes. Using morphOTU, we 29 

derive image-based OTUs across five standardized benchmark datasets spanning 30 

flowers, wood anatomy, and beetle dorsal habitus. These units closely approximate 31 

reference species-level groupings, including closely related species, retain coherent 32 

structure when most species are “unseen’’ during training, and accurately approximate 33

Introduction 46 

Biodiversity underpins the functioning, stability, and resilience of Earth’s ecosystems, 47 

yet our ability to quantify it remains fundamentally constrained—despite the growing 48 

need for a deeper understanding driven by ecosystem services (Millennium 49 

Ecosystem Assessment 2005; Cardinale et al. 2012), economic value (de Groot et al. 50 

2012; Costanza et al. 1997, 2014), links to human health (Hanski et al. 2012; Civitello 51 

et al. 2015; Keesing & Ostfeld 2000, 2021), and ethical or cultural motivations 52 

(Rolston 1988; Berkes et al. 2017). Taxonomy forms the foundational infrastructure of 53 

biodiversity science: the hierarchical structure of the Linnaean system (Linnaeus 1758) 54 

and the standardization provided by international codes (ICZN; ICNafp; ICNB; ICTV) 55 

enable biological data to be acquired, managed, compared, and shared as structured 56 

scientific knowledge. 57 

Despite centuries of progress, traditional morphology-based taxonomy faces the 58 

well-documented challenges of the taxonomic impediment (Hoagland 1996; Evenhuis 59 

2007; Hortal et al. 2015), entailing extremely high species diversity, strong 60

transferability across taxa. Whether considering the ~2.2 million reported species 62 

(Bánki et al. 2026) or the millions or even tens of millions yet to be described (Mora 63 

et al. 2011; Stork 2018; Wiens 2023), classical taxonomy struggles to meet the 64 

identification demands of modern biodiversity research. Taxonomic chauvinism 65 

further biases biodiversity knowledge toward vertebrates or large flowering plants 66 

(Bonnet et al. 2002; Feeley et al. 2016; Groom et al. 2025), diminishing the scientific 67 

value of datasets by marginalizing the overwhelmingly diverse invertebrate fauna. In 68 

current workflows, unnamed or unidentified individuals remain “silent” in data 69 

systems; because categorization into units (typically species) is a hard prerequisite for 70 

downstream ecological analyses and policy-based management, biodiversity 71 

quantification and protection become tightly coupled to taxonomic completeness. 72 

Molecular approaches such as DNA barcoding (Hebert et al. 2003) and 73 

metabarcoding (Taberlet et al. 2012) overcome many morphological limitations 74 

through standardized genetic markers, high throughput, lower expertise requirements, 75 

and fine taxonomic resolution. They support distance-based species delimitation, the 76 

concept of molecular operational taxonomic units (OTUs) (Callahan et al. 2016; 77 

Edgar 2016), flexible sampling from individuals or environmental mixtures 78 

(Hajibabaei et al. 2016; Alberdi et al. 2017), and DNA acquisition from tissues or 79 

eDNA (Orgiazzi et al. 2014), enabling full-spectrum biodiversity detection from 80 

individuals to communities (Cristescu 2014; DeSalle & Goldstein 2019). Nevertheless, 81 

molecular workflows are not without limits: laboratory infrastructure, strict sample 82 

preservation, and issues such as primer bias (Leray et al. 2013; Elbrecht & Leese 2017; 83 

Alberdi et al. 2017; Ruppert et al. 2019; Rishan et al. 2023) restrict their applicability, 84 

and sequencing costs remain non-trivial for large-scale work (Stein et al. 2014). 85 

Incomplete reference databases (Keck et al. 2022) give rise to large numbers of 86 

unnamed “dark taxa” (Hartop et al. 2022; Nilsson et al. 2023), which—although 87 

analyzable as OTUs—remain unusable in conservation policy or species-level 88 

ecological interpretation. The genetic-only paradigm also deepens the disconnect 89

Machine-learning approaches grounded in computer vision have recently 92 

emerged as promising tools for automating biodiversity monitoring. Deep learning 93 

can detect taxonomically diagnostic morphological traits (Hansen et al. 2020; Zhao et 94 

al. 2023; Rayeed et al. 2025) and may match or exceed expert identification 95 

performance (Valan et al. 2019). Multimodal classification combining morphology 96 

and molecules can further enhance accuracy (Yang et al. 2022; Hofmann et al. 2024; 97 

Karbstein et al. 2024; Kösters et al. 2025; Hunt et al. 2025). However, most existing 98 

models operate under closed-set supervised learning: they require predefined species 99 

labels, their training labels mirror traditional taxonomy, and they cannot handle the 100 

substantial unseen or undescribed diversity present in real ecosystems. Moreover, 101 

supervised models usually rely on background cues rather than organismal 102 

phenotypes (Geirhos et al. 2020; Caron et al. 2021). Zero-shot and few-shot methods 103 

may generalize to genus-level or higher (Stevens et al. 2023; Gu et al. 2025; Rayeed 104 

et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2026), but still cannot delineate species-level entities or define 105 

operational units. Without solving open-set recognition, machine learning cannot 106 

generate truly actionable biodiversity data. 107 

We propose that overcoming this impasse requires a shift in perspective: 108 

phenotypic diversity can be measured directly within a continuous morphological 109 

space, independent of species identities. By treating morphology as a 110 

high-dimensional, organism-centered signal—rather than a set of discrete 111 

classification labels—image-derived phenotypes can serve as a broadly applicable 112 

substrate for biodiversity quantification. Such a framework should (i) operate under 113 

fully open-set conditions, (ii) integrate both described and undescribed taxa, and (iii) 114 

exploit rapidly expanding global digital biodiversity resources. Yet no existing 115 

approach provides a general, scalable, and interpretable pathway for constructing 116 

morphology-based operational taxonomic units parallel to molecular OTUs. 117 

Here, we introduce morphOTU, a general and high-throughput workflow for 118

strategy to transform image-only, DNA-free, and often poorly annotated 122

biodiversity survey images—into biologically interpretable morphOTUs. The 124 

morphOTU workflow demonstrates stable and reliable image-derived OTU formation 125 

under both supervised learning and combined self-supervised-plus-fine-tuning 126

and beetle datasets covering heterogeneous imaging conditions, morphOTUs align 128 

with expert-defined species labels under limited-label and open-set conditions. 129

diversity indices. Visual explanations show that OTU-Former focuses on biologically 131 

meaningful traits such as body outlines, sculpture, floral symmetry, and wood vessel 132 

architecture, enabling expert validation. Importantly, morphOTUs also remain robust 133 

when applied to a real-world biodiversity survey dataset, demonstrating the feasibility 134 

of generating abundance-based morphological OTUs beyond standardized imaging 135 

environments. Together, these results establish MorphOTU as a scalable, image-based 136 

framework capable of generating abundance-based morphological OTUs under 137 

real-world ecological conditions. 138

Benchmarking MorphOTUs on closed-set datasets 178 

Across all five closed-set datasets, we systematically evaluated four 179 

embedding-learning paradigms: (1) pretrained backbone models, (2) supervised 180 

fine-tuned backbones (SL), (3) self-supervised learning (SSL), and (4) supervised 181 

fine-tuning applied to SSL features (SSL+SFT). Our SSL encoder is a lightweight 182 

architecture specifically designed for small- to medium-scale datasets (typically up to 183 

100k images), and, when combined with ArcFace fine-tuning (Deng et al. 2022), 184 

forms the OTU-Former module (Fig. 1b). To assess model-family differences 185 

independent of training strategy, we tested a diverse panel of CNN and Transformer 186 

backbones: CNNs (ResNet-50, ConvNeXt-v2, RegNetY) (He et al. 2016; Woo et al. 187 

2023; Radosavovic et al. 2020), and Vision Transformers (ViT, EVA02, DINOv3) 188 

(Dosovitskiy et al. 2020; Fang et al. 2024; Siméoni et al. 2025). Except for 189 

OTU-Former, which uses ViT exclusively, we trained each architecture under all four 190 

learning paradigms. This design allowed us to compare the relative contributions of 191 

backbone choice and training objective to embedding quality. 192

Across all five closed-set datasets, we evaluated embedding quality using three 207 

representative metrics: classification transferability (LP accuracy), retrieval 208 

performance (mAP), and clustering quality (ARI). Embeddings extracted from 209 

pretrained backbone models already performed well on Flowers-102, likely reflecting 210 

the strong overlap between this dataset and the large-scale image corpora used during 211 

pretraining. A summary of these performance improvements across the four 212 

embedding-learning paradigms is shown in Fig. 2a. 213 

For Flowers-102, further fine-tuning offered limited additional benefit in this 214 

case, and we therefore did not conduct SSL experiments. In contrast, embeddings 215 

from WOOD and ZZH-Lucaninae were moderate in quality, whereas Rove-Tree-11 216 

and NHM-Carabids showed substantially lower performance (e.g., LP accuracy < 0.5, 217 

mAP < 0.3, ARI < 0.35; Fig. S1). As expected, ResNet-50 consistently lagged behind 218 

the other backbones, while the remaining pretrained CNN and Transformer models 219 

showed no decisive winner. 220 

Supervised fine-tuning using AutoGluon substantially improved embedding 221 

quality across nearly all backbones and datasets. Except for RegNetY-400MF, all 222 

models achieved LP accuracy > 0.95, mAP > 0.85, and ARI > 0.7 across the five 223 

datasets (Fig. S2). ConvNeXt-v2 and EVA02 exhibited the strongest performance 224 

overall; remarkably, even their smallest variants (~5M parameters; ConvNeXt-v2-F 225 

and EVA02-T) maintained most metrics above 0.95. 226 

Self-supervised learning (SSL) provided a notable improvement over pretrained 227 

backbones, with LP accuracy increases exceeding 0.2 on several datasets and 228 

approaching 1.0 on WOOD. As dataset difficulty increased, the relative gains 229 

achieved by SSL diminished, indicating that datasets of varying complexity offer 230

To characterize embedding geometry, we computed pairwise cosine distances 240 

between all images. Distance ranges were largely determined by model family: 241 

RegNetY and ResNet-50 produced narrower ranges (0–1), substantially lower than 242 

other models (0–1.5; Fig. S5). SSL distances were generally slightly smaller than SL 243 

or SSL+SFT (Fig. S6), likely because supervised fine-tuning explicitly enlarges 244 

interclass margins (Pan et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024). Unlike molecular genetic 245 

distances, which often display a well-defined “barcoding gap”, cosine-distance 246 

distributions from image embeddings exhibited no single, discrete separation (Fig. 2b; 247 

Figs. S7–S16). Instead, they showed at most a shallow trough, consistent with 248 

lineage-specific variability in morphological divergence. UMAP projections of the 249 

same embeddings nevertheless showed well-defined species-level structure under 250 

SSL+SFT (Fig. 2c). Intra-class distances remained tightly constrained, with mean 251 

values typically within approximately 0.3 (Fig. S17). These patterns jointly indicate 252 

that although fixed global thresholds are inappropriate, morphology-based OTU 253 

formation remains feasible when adaptive, lineage-specific thresholds are used. 254

thresholds determine morphOTU boundaries. As shown in Fig. 3a, no single cutoff 256 

yields perfect partitions: some thresholds oversplit species (P1–3), whereas others 257 

lump several species into one OTU (P2–4). Ideally, final decisions would incorporate 258 

expert evaluation or CAM-based guidance, using multiple partitioning schemes and 259

Overall, these results demonstrate that morphological barcodes produced by both 274 

SL and SSL-based training regimes are stable and intrinsically clusterable. When 275 

paired with dynamic, lineage-specific distance thresholds—and optionally guided by 276 

expert inspection—the resulting morphOTUs approximate reference species-level 277 

groupings with high fidelity. Even when using a single operational cutoff within each 278 

dataset, morphOTU-based diversity indices remain usable and closely match the 279 

expert-assigned species labels, confirming the feasibility of morphology-driven OTU 280 

construction on closed-set datasets. 281

Open-set with “unseen” species 293 

Real-world biodiversity surveys frequently involve open-set conditions in which 294 

many encountered species are absent from the training data: a scenario especially 295 

common in invertebrate groups (Stork 2018). To evaluate how morphOTU behaves 296 

under such conditions, we examined the generalization performance of both SL and 297 

SSL+SFT embeddings as the number of open-set species increased, thereby 298 

characterizing the framework's scaling behavior. We constructed four gradients in 299 

which unseen species constituted 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% of all images. SL and 300 

SSL+SFT performed comparably across these settings. 301 

As expected, embedding quality decreased as the proportion of open-set data 302 

increased. When unseen species increased to 75% of all images, leaving only 25% 303 

represented in training, LP accuracy dropped to 0.66–0.81, mAP to 0.45–0.56, and 304 

ARI to 0.44–0.46 (Figs. S19, S20). Pairwise cosine distances and intra-class distances 305 

also became slightly more variable as unseen species increased (Figs. S21, S22), 306 

indicating a loosening of embedding compactness. Nevertheless, the overall 307 

embedding geometry remained structured, suggesting that meaningful clusters can 308 

still be recovered even when the majority of species are not present during training. 309 

When morphOTUs were generated using a single distance threshold, OTU 310 

counts increased relative to baseline models trained under closed-set conditions. After 311 

abundance filtering, ZZH-Lucaninae and Rove-Tree-11 retained richness estimates 312 

similar to their closed-set baselines, whereas NHM-Carabids exhibited more than a 313 

two-fold increase in estimated OTU richness. Shannon diversity derived from 314

Sparse labeled supervision 322 

In the scaling-law tests above, labeled data and training data were identical. However, 323 

in practical self-supervised learning workflows, the amount of labeled material is 324 

typically far smaller than the amount of imagery available for training. This 325 

corresponds to a common biodiversity scenario in which large numbers of specimens 326 

can be incorporated into pretraining (without labels), but only a limited fraction can 327 

be taxonomically identified. To assess the impact of labeled data availability on the 328 

ArcFace SFT stage, we varied the size of the annotated subset while keeping SSL 329 

pretraining fixed. Experiments were conducted on the Dun25 setting, with 75% of 330 

images used for SSL pretraining and the remaining 25% unseen species held out as 331 

open-set test data. We then fine-tuned ArcFace using 75% (as in the pretraining), 50%, 332 

25%, or 10% of the labeled portion. 333 

Even with only 10% labeled data for SFT, embeddings remained stable, 334 

achieving LP accuracy > 0.65, mAP > 0.44, and ARI > 0.41. Pairwise cosine distances 335 

and intra-class distances also remained consistent across label fractions (Figs. S19, 336 

S20). Diversity estimates behaved similarly to the open-set results above: richness and 337 

Shannon indices derived from morphOTUs remained close to expert-assigned species 338 

labels (Fig. S18). 339 

These results show that, given strong SSL pretraining, extensive high-quality 340 

taxonomic annotation is not essential. Moderate labeled fractions (10%–25% in this 341 

study) are sufficient to preserve good generalization performance, in line with 342

Discussion 545 

A new generalizable workflow for image-based biodiversity discovery 546 

Our morphology-based OTU workflow represents more than a technical improvement 547 

in species identification: it signals a broader conceptual shift toward scalable, 548 

data-driven phenotyping in biodiversity research. By integrating self-supervised 549 

feature learning with lineage-specific clustering, we can convert raw images into 550 

standardized, abundance-based morphOTUs, reducing reliance on expert-driven 551 

labeling and enabling biodiversity assessment at scales previously unattainable via 552 

morphology. This transition parallels the transformative impact that DNA barcoding 553 

and metabarcoding had on molecular ecology two decades ago. 554 

Beyond these operational advantages, morphOTU helps mitigate the 555 

long-standing taxonomic impediment by reducing dependence on complete expert 556 

identification and enabling preliminary organization of poorly annotated specimens 557 

(Hoagland 1996; Evenhuis 2007; Hortal et al. 2015). Although performance still 558 

benefits from sufficient organismal visibility and image quality, the framework can 559 

operate on moderately heterogeneous image collections. Different from many 560 

previous deep-learning approaches, which depend on closed-set supervised 561 

classification and therefore are difficult to apply under biodiversity conditions with 562 

scarce labels (Hansen et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2023; Rayeed et al. 2025), morphOTU 563 

uses self-supervised learning to extract organism-centered features while reducing 564 

reliance on background artifacts (Geirhos et al. 2020; Caron et al. 2021). This enables 565

scalable, open-set phenotyping even when most species remain unidentified. This 566 

claim is further supported by our real-world evaluation on the GD-Insect dataset. 567 

During supervised fine-tuning, only 28 species (10.41%) were labeled and included in 568 

training, while the remaining approximately 90% of species were entirely unseen. The 569 

model constructed morphOTUs from a mixed test set containing both seen and unseen 570 

taxa, demonstrating stable clustering and consistent phenotypic grouping under 571 

realistic, partially labeled open-set conditions. 572 

Unlike classical morphology-based workflows, morphOTU can operate on 573 

moderately heterogeneous image collections, but its performance benefits from 574 

sufficient organismal visibility, resolution, and standardized imaging. Because 575 

morphOTUs can be generated with limited expert labels, the framework may reduce 576 

the amount of taxonomic training required for preliminary sorting and diversity 577 

assessment. The ability to visualize diagnostic structures through activation maps is 578 

particularly significant: continuous morphological traits, which are foundational but 579 

challenging to formalize, become directly observable and computationally accessible. 580 

Previous deep-learning approaches have been constrained by closed-set assumptions 581 

and limited interpretability; in contrast, morphOTU learns organism-centered features 582 

in an open-set framework, achieving supervised-level performance while maintaining 583 

robustness in systems with many undescribed taxa. 584 

Conceptually, morphOTUs function as morphological analogues to molecular 585 

OTUs and metabarcoding-derived units (Hebert et al. 2003; Taberlet et al. 2012). 586 

They provide a non-destructive, infrastructure-light representation of phenotypic 587 

space without requiring sequencing infrastructure or per-sample sequencing costs 588 

(Stein et al. 2014). Rather than replacing molecular data, morphOTUs complement 589 

and extend existing taxonomic practice: they offer rapid preliminary sorting, reveal 590 

phenotypic structure invisible to coarse categorical traits, and flag specimens that 591 

merit targeted sequencing. This integrative workflow aligns morphology, molecules, 592 

and machine learning into a coherent system for biodiversity discovery, supporting 593 

taxonomic revision and enhancing the digitization and curation of natural history 594

No universal distance threshold delineates morphOTUs across all lineages 596 

Although conceptually parallel to molecular OTUs, morphOTUs differ fundamentally 597 

in their statistical properties. Molecular systems often exhibit a broad barcoding gap 598 

separating intra- and interspecific divergence (Hebert et al. 2004; Smith et al. 2005), 599 

typically around a COI threshold of 0.03 (Hebert et al. 2003), although overlap 600 

between classes may occur (Meyer & Paulay 2005; Wiemers & Fiedler 2007). 601 

Contrary to this, morphological distances widely vary within species and show 602 

substantial overlap across species. In our datasets, intra-class morphological variation 603 

often spanned cosine-distance ranges up to 0.3, illustrating that image-embedding 604 

distances do not exhibit the same threshold behavior as COI sequence divergences, 605 

reflecting the evolutionary nature of phenotypic variation (e.g., Fig. S7). Some 606 

lineages diversify through discrete, readily visible traits, while others exhibit 607 

continuous variation shaped by adaptation, sexual selection, or phenotypic plasticity. 608 

Consequently, no universal morphological threshold can reliably delineate 609 

species-level units, and morphological barcodes do not exhibit clean “gaps” (Fig. 2b). 610 

Our dynamic-thresholding strategy accommodates this heterogeneity by 611 

generating multiple partitioning schemes, enabling stable clusters to be identified 612 

across thresholds and corroborated using Score-CAM visualizations or taxonomic 613 

knowledge (Fig. 3a). Although absolute OTU boundaries vary with threshold choice, 614 

community-level diversity metrics derived from single-threshold morphOTUs were 615 

robust, suggesting that flexible thresholding need not impede ecological inference. 616 

Imaging conditions can also influence MorphOTU counts. Variations in 617 

background, orientation, illumination, or effective resolution can produce long 618 

branches or weakly supported nodes in UPGMA trees, highlighting individuals 619 

requiring expert review or molecular validation. The NHM-Carabids dataset (Hansen 620 

et al. 2020) underscores this: despite its size, lower image quality reduced embedding 621 

clarity and increased uncertainty, consistent with the reliance of self-supervised 622

et al. 2023) (Figs. S23 −S27). These findings reinforce the value of standardized 624 

imaging, particularly when leveraging self-supervised representation learning, which 625 

benefits strongly from high-quality data. Although standardized imaging improves 626 

overall consistency, our degradation experiment demonstrates that the primary 627 

limitation for self-supervised representation learning is image quality, particularly 628 

organismal visibility and structural detail, rather than standardization alone. This 629 

distinction explains why the NHM-Carabids dataset, which contains many low-detail, 630 

small-scale specimens, yields weaker representations despite its relatively uniform 631 

imaging protocol. 632 

These observations argue for a conceptual shift away from fixed-threshold 633 

morphology and toward multi-resolution, expert-informed frameworks. Such an 634 

approach is more faithful to the evolutionary complexity of phenotypes and more 635 

suited to the diversity of organisms encountered in large-scale biodiversity surveys. 636 

Limitations and prospects 637 

Although morphOTU demonstrates broad applicability, several limitations point to 638 

opportunities for future development. As with molecular OTUs, morphological 639 

heterogeneity across lineages makes it difficult for a single external marker, for 640 

example, the dorsal view, to capture the full range of diagnostic traits. Integrating 641 

multiple views, combining several morphological markers, or leveraging emerging 642 

3D and multiview technologies could significantly enhance morphOTU resolution 643 

and improve generality across taxa. The taxonomic coverage and imaging diversity of 644 

our current datasets remain limited, underscoring the need for broader evaluation 645 

across additional lineages and imaging contexts. Moreover, applying morphOTU to 646 

holometabolous insects, where larval and adult stages exhibit drastically different 647 

morphologies, remains challenging and requires future methodological extensions. 648 

From a technological perspective, morphOTU was optimized for medium-sized 649 

datasets and accessible hardware. As visual foundation models continue to evolve, 650

Expanding training to include diverse environments, imaging styles, and taxonomic 653

Looking ahead, image-based morphOTUs have the potential to reshape 655

phenotypic and genetic dimensions of organismal diversity across ecosystems, time 661

Model architectures 751 

We evaluated three categories of image-embedding extraction methods: (i) pretrained 752 

backbone models without task-specific training, (ii) supervised fine-tuning using 753 

AutoGluon-Multimodal (AutoMM), and (iii) the OTU-Former architecture combining 754 

self-supervised pretraining and supervised metric learning. 755 

Pretrained backbone models 756 

As baseline representations, we used the script, autogluon_training.py, to extract 757 

embeddings directly from five ImageNet-scale pretrained backbones (base-level 758 

parameter size, ~85M): two CNNs (RegNetY-16GF, ConvNeXt V2-B), two 759 

transformer models (ViT-B, EVA02-B), and one self-supervised model (DINOv3-B). 760 

ResNet-50 (He et al. 2016) was included as an additional reference backbone. 761

Supervised learning with AutoMM 762 

Supervised models were trained using AutoGluon-Multimodal (AutoMM) (Tang et al. 763 

2024). For each dataset, images were split using stratified sampling (90% 764 

train+validation, 10% test). Data augmentation followed AutoMM’s default pipeline, 765 

including resizing, center cropping, horizontal/vertical flips, color jitter, and trivial 766 

augment. Unless otherwise noted, the maximum training run was 50 epochs, using 767 

cross-entropy loss. After fine-tuning, embeddings were extracted from the penultimate 768 

layer of the model. All supervised procedures were implemented from Zhang (2026). 769 

OTU-Former architecture 770 

OTU-Former consists of a self-supervised pretraining (SSL) stage and a supervised 771 

fine-tuning (SFT) stage. A ViT-based encoder serves as the backbone, using the CLS 772 

token as the image-level embedding. 773 

Self-supervised pretraining 774 

The SSL stage employs a teacher–student framework updated by an exponential 775 

moving average (EMA). The student network comprises a ViT encoder followed by a 776 

3-layer MLP projector producing fixed-dimensional L2-normalized embeddings. The 777 

teacher and student receive multi-crop views of each image with geometric and 778 

appearance augmentations. 779 

The SSL objective combines three losses: 780 

1. Global self-distillation loss between teacher and student CLS embeddings, 781 

2. Local-to-global distillation loss , aligning local student crops to teacher global 782 

embeddings, 783 

3. Masked token regression loss , predicting teacher patch-token outputs for 784 

masked positions. 785 

Learning rate schedules, EMA momentum, and temperature warmup followed 786 

cosine or linear schedules as specified in Supplementary Materials. 787

Supervised fine-tuning 788 

The SFT stage applies ArcFace metric learning (Deng et al. 2022) to refine class 789 

separation. For fine-tuning, the first 70% of transformer blocks were frozen, and the 790 

remaining blocks and metric head were trained. The final OTU-Former embedding is 791 

a fixed-length vector. 792 

Embedding evaluation 793 

Embeddings were evaluated using classification and clustering benchmarks, including 794 

k-nearest neighbor accuracy, linear probing performance, retrieval metrics (Recall@K, 795 

mAP), and clustering indices (NMI, ARI, silhouette score, purity). Additional metrics 796 

for metric-learning models (intra-class variance, inter-class distance, embedding 797 

norms) and UMAP visualizations (McInnes et al. 2018) were computed using 798 

standard implementations. Linear probe accuracy was used as a post hoc measure of 799 

embedding separability rather than as an operational open-set classifier. For open-set 800 

experiments, the probe was trained and evaluated on labeled evaluation splits that 801 

included species withheld from representation learning, allowing assessment of 802 

whether unseen species formed linearly separable clusters. Retrieval metrics and 803 

clustering indices were computed using reference labels only for evaluation. For 804 

GD-Insect, singleton species were retained for morphOTU richness and 805 

Shannon-diversity calculations but excluded from metrics requiring train–test or 806 

positive-pair evaluation, including linear probing and mAP. ARI was calculated over 807 

all specimens using expert-assigned species labels unless otherwise specified. 808

Partitioning across distance thresholds 815 

To obtain clusterings at multiple resolutions, we generated a series of partitioning 816 

schemes by applying distance cutoffs across the same UPGMA tree. For each cutoff, 817

statistics describing the distribution of clade sizes. Clade stability was assessed using 819 

bootstrap resampling of the original embeddings. 820 

Evaluation of partition quality 821 

Partition quality was quantified using clustering-based and label-based indices, 822 

including normalized mutual information (NMI), adjusted mutual information (AMI), 823 

adjusted Rand index (ARI), silhouette score, purity, homogeneity, completeness, 824 

V-measure, and BCubed precision/recall/F-score. Additional summaries, including 825 

splitting and lumping indices and the proportion of monophyletic groups, were 826 

computed from the partition assignments. Diversity estimates (richness and Shannon 827 

indices) were calculated under alternative filtering schemes, with optional exclusion 828 

of low-abundance morphOTUs. 829

Experimental scenarios 841 

We evaluated morphOTU performance under multiple controlled experimental 842 

scenarios designed to reflect conditions commonly encountered in image-based 843 

biodiversity assessments. All scenarios were applied using the same embedding 844 

extraction and clustering procedures described above. Unless otherwise noted, 845 

supervised learning (SL) and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) used stratified dataset 846 

splits of 90% for training and 10% for testing. 847 

Baseline closed-set evaluation 848 

Baseline experiments assessed model performance when all test classes were also 849 

present in the training set. For SL, we used four backbone families—ConvNeXt V2, 850 

RegNetY, ViT, and EVA02—each represented by three parameter-scale variants (large, 851 

medium, and small; Table S2). For SSL and SSL+SFT, we used ViT-T, ViT-S, and 852 

ViT-B as backbones. SSL was trained on all available images for each dataset; during 853 

SFT, 90% of the images were used for fine-tuning, with the remaining 10% reserved 854 

for testing. 855

Impact of labeled-data availability 863 

To examine the dependence of SFT on labeled sample size, we varied the proportion 864 

of labeled images available during SFT while holding the SSL stage constant. Using 865 

the Dun25 split as the base scenario (25% open-set test, 75% used for SSL), we 866

original size, generating the nested datasets Dun25, Dun25_FT50, Dun25_FT25, and 868 

Dun25_FT10. SSL was conducted using all images available under the Dun25 869 

scenario, while only the subsampled labeled fractions were used in the ArcFace SFT 870 

stage. 871 

Limited images per species 872 

To evaluate the effect of reduced within-class sample diversity, we controlled the 873 

number of training images per species while keeping the set of species fixed. Using 874 

the NHM-Carabids dataset as an example, species with at least 105 images were 875 

designated as the closed-set. For each species, we randomly sampled 100, 50, 25, or 876 

10 images for training. Remaining images from these species formed the closed-set 877 

test subset, while all other species served as open-set test classes. This produced the 878 

nested datasets Dsub100, Dsub50, Dsub25, and Dsub10 (Table S1). 879

Low-quality images in training 887 

To quantitatively assess the effect of image degradation on representation learning, we 888 

conducted a controlled downsampling experiment using the high-quality 889 

ZZH-Lucaninae dataset. Images were resized to fixed short-edge lengths of 512, 256, 890 

128, 64, and 32 pixels to simulate progressively lower levels of visual detail. For each 891 

resolution, SL, SSL, and SSL+SFT models were trained independently using the 892 

corresponding downsampled images. Embedding quality was evaluated using the 893 

original 512-pixel test images to ensure comparability across conditions. 894 

Because real-world image degradation can arise from multiple factors, including 895 

focus, illumination, contrast, motion blur, and specimen size within the frame, 896 

short-edge downsampling serves as a controlled and quantifiable proxy rather than a 897 

full representation of all quality loss modes. Nevertheless, this procedure provides a 898 

standardized way to examine how reduced image resolution influences the ability of 899 

different learning paradigms to extract stable morphological features. 900
