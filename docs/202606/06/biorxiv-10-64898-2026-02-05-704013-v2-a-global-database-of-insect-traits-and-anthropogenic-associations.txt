Abstract 30 

Insect research remains hindered by limited data availability and fragmented knowledge 31 

compared to other, better -documented taxonomic groups.  Yet , both the macroecological and 32 

the insect research communities highlight the need to integrate large -scale ecological trait 33 

datasets for insects . Insects and humans are interconnected through diverse relationships ,34 

ranging from  beneficial interactions, such as the  use  of insects as  nutritional  resources, to 35 

adverse  impacts, including their  role in  to the emergence and spread of biological invasions. 36 

Understanding the traits of insects associated with human use, movement and impact is 37 

therefore important for linking insects to ecosystem function and global change.  We present 38 

AnthropInsect , one of  the largest database on insect traits to date, which uniquely includes 39 

variables describing human -insect associations.  AnthropInsect  describes species through 35 40 

variables grouped into five categories: (i) taxonomic descriptors; (ii) ecological descriptors 41 

(native bioregions and habitat); (iii) human -insect associations (edibility and invasive status); 42 

(i v) functional traits (behavior, morphology, life history and feeding); (v) and macroecological 43 

descriptors of native -range geography and climate.  AnthropInsect  currently includes  5867 44 

species across six major orders: Coleoptera, Lepidoptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Orthoptera 45 

and Blattodea. Data extracted from peer -reviewed , grey literature and from existing databases 46 

were standardized and  validated  with expert knowledge to ensure accuracy. By providing traits 47 

data with information on insect –human interactions, this rigorously curated resource supports 48 

global research in entomology, ecology, conservation, and global change. 49 

Keywords:  AnthopInsect,  edible insects, invasive species, ecological traits, macroecological 50 

indicators, functional descriptors 51 

Background & Summary 52 

Over the last decades, there has been increasing recognition and consensus that biodiversity is 53 

multi -dimensional, encompassing taxonomic, phylogenetic, and functional  diversities , all of 54 

which are essential to consider for effective conservation strategies 1–4 . Trait -based approaches 55 

have therefore become central in ecology and are now applied across a broad range of taxa 5–8.56 

Large curated and harmonized trait databases have been developed, enabling comparative 57 

studies of macroecological patterns and trends.  These initiatives have focused particularly on 58 

plants and vertebrates, with notable e xamples includ ing  TRY, a global database of plant traits 9;59 

AmphiBIO, which compiles natural history traits for amphibians worldwide 10 ; FISHMORPH, 60 

a database of fish morphological traits 11 ; COMBINE, a consolidated database of intrinsic and 61

all bird species 13 . In contrast, other taxonomic groups,  particularly  insects, still suffer from 63 

major gaps in trait information and  understanding  of  their ecological functions, a limitation 64 

known as the Raukiærian shortfall 14 –18 .65 

In addition to being the most speciose and abundant class of animals, insects are also 66 

functionally diverse and fulfil key ecological roles, including pollination, nutrient cycles, seed 67 

dispersal, and ecosystem engineering 19,20 . Through their  diverse  trophic functions  (e.g. ,68 

herbivores, decomposers, predators, and parasitoids), functional niches and  traits, they regulate 69 

plant and arthropod populations and  constitute a strong basis of  many trophic networks 21 –23 . As 70 

these ecological functions are essential to  ecosystem functioning and human well -being, insects 71 

represent a priority group for macroecological and conservation studies 24 . In response, 72 

substantial efforts have been made by the scientific community  and initiatives have generated 73 

order -, family -, region -, or ecosystem -specific insect trait datasets. These include datasets on 74 

North American lotic insects traits and their evolutionary associations 25 ; ants traits with 75 

associated georeferenced assemblage -level data at the global scale 26 ; odonates phenotypic 76 

traits 27 ; European aquatic macroinvertebrates and their dispersal traits 28 ; heteroptera traits from 77 

a protected area of China 29 ; and bees 30 . However, existing resources remain highly specific and 78 

insufficiently integrated, limiting cross -taxonomic and cross -regional comparisons, while 79 

available trait data remain fragmented and lack centralization 26,27 . To address this challenge, 80 

Cardoso et al. 31  called for the establishment of a global network of collaborators to collectively 81 

accelerate the compilation of insect trait data and develop a unified insect trait database, 82 

providing guidelines and a framework for such a database. 83 

Insects  are  often closely linked to and influenced by anthropogenic activities, with interactions 84 

that can have both positive and negative effects on human well -being.  On the one hand, they 85 

can provide valuable resources,  acting as biological control  agents  of pests or contributing  to 86 

medical applications 32,33 . On the other hand,  some  insects play key roles in global change 87 

processes, which are major drivers of global biodiversity loss . Among these processes, 88 

biological invasions  - defined here as the human -mediated introduction of species beyond their 89 

historical ranges  - represent a major driver of global biodiversity loss 4,34,35 . Insects are the most 90 

frequently introduced taxonomic group and one of the most invasive taxa globally 36 –38 . Their 91 

introductions can have substantial impacts on ecosystems, human well -being and health, and 92 

economic activities 39,40 . Insect introductions are increasing with globalization, many of which 93 

are linked to human use and trade. This tight relationship is particularly  important in the case 94

With  the recent rise of  insect farming for food and feed, several studies have highlighted the 102 

risk that edible species  raised for industrial purposes  could contribute to new biological 103 

invasions when farmed outside their native ranges 46 –48 . We created  AnthropInsect , a database 104 

focused on insect species relevant to human activities, illustrated here through both negative 105 

(invasive species) and positive (edible species) associations . This database  provides trait 106 

coverage for  5867  species spanning six of the most speciose insect orders (Coleoptera, 107 

Lepidoptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Orthoptera, and Blattodea) 49 . It  compiles  35 variables 108 

describing  taxonomic, ecological, functional, human -association and macroecological 109 

dimensions , which are often linked to the processes driving biological invasions 45,50 .110 

AnthropInsect  is a standalone database  that encompasses key dimensions for understanding 111 

insect traits in an increasingly human -dominated world. It is also interoperable and  can  naturally 112 

complement and  be  integrate d with  the  Global  Repository  of Insect Traits ( GRIT ) initiative 113 

proposed by Cardoso et al. 31 . As an open, evolving, and community -driven resource, 114 

AnthropInsect  is designed to support large -scale comparative studies in entomology, invasion 115 

biology, climate change research, functional ecology, and applied sciences. 116

Selection of variables and trait data extraction 165 

We described each  insect  species in the database using 35 variables organised into five 166 

categories ( Table 1 ): (i) taxonomic descriptors (9 variables; order to species level, including 167 

the GBIF backbone taxon ID); (ii) ecological descriptors (2 variables; native bioregions, and 168 

larval and adult habitats); (iii) human associations (2 variables; edibility and invasion status); 169 

(iv) functional traits (18 variables) describing life histories, behavior, feeding, and morphology; 170 

and (v) macroecological variables (4 variables) capturing native -range geographic distribution 171 

and climatic conditions (temperature and humidity). 172 

With the exception of macroecological variables, we compiled trait information primarily from 173 

peer -reviewed and grey literature sources. While this approach demanded substantial 174 

taxonomic expertise, it greatly contributed to  improving  data completeness and consistency 175 

across the database , with various experts  also  contributing their own unpublished field data .176 

We assigned traits values using three documented approaches: (i) direct extraction: we extracted 177 

values from the literature and, where available, from existing databases (e.g. ,58 –60 ); (ii) expert 178 

assignment: we assigned values based on expert knowledge for variables that are well 179 

documented and conservative at higher taxonomic levels (e.g., metamorphosis type at the order 180 

or family level) ; and (iii) phylogenetic inference: we inferred values from the closest related 181 

taxa when species -level information was unavailable, prioritising congeners (same genus). We 182 

applied this inference mainly to Orthoptera and only rarely to other groups. For each record, 183 

the database reports the assignment method and the corresponding source information to ensure 184 

full traceability. 185

with multiple invasion -status categories, we restricted native -range macroecological descriptors 191 

to occurrence records within each species’ native bioregions. Additionally, we excluded records 192 

located outside  main land s (i.e., in marine environments) . Lastly, we  computed four native -193 

range macroecological descriptors ( Table 1 ): (i) latitude range, defined as the difference 194 

between maximum and minimum latitudes (decimal degrees); (ii) longitude range, defined as 195 

the difference between maximum and minimum longitudes (decimal degrees); (iii) thermal 196

the range, respectively ; and (iv) humidity range, defined as the difference between maximum 199 

and minimum humidities (%, hurs_max and hurs_min) extracted  from  occurrence locations 200 

corresponding to the wettest and driest points of the range, respectively . For species with more 201 

than 10 retained occurrences, we reduced the influence of extreme values by trimming the 202

Possible application 216 

By integrating insect trait data with human -related and macroecological information, 217 

AnthropInsect  fills  critical knowledge gaps across a wide range of disciplines, including 218 

entomology, invasion biology, climate change, functional ecology, and applied sciences. 219 

AnthropInsect  follows the FAIR principles which make it readily  integra ble  into the larger 220 

Global Repository of Insect Traits initiated  by Cardoso et al. 31 , thereby contributing to a global 221 

collaborative network aimed at accelerating the compilation of insect trait data and improving 222 

transparency, accessibility, and data sharing within the field. 223 

Currently, the database focuses on edibility and invasive status , which are likely among the 224 

most important species groups.  However, human associations span a broader scope of possible 225 

species groups, which can be integrated in the database in the future . These include  medicinal 226 

uses, agricultural (native) pests, bioindicators, disease vectors, cultural significance (e.g., 227 

ecotourism, symbolism), ecosystem services, and material uses (e.g., honey, silk, dyes, wax). 228 

Data  records 229 

The current release of  AnthropInsect  comprises  5867  insect species described by 35 variables 230 

grouped into five categories: (i) taxonomic descriptors, (ii) ecological descriptors, (iii) human 231 

associations, (iv) functional traits, and (v) macroecological variables. 232 

The  AnthropInsect  database is publicly available to facilitate access and reuse. It has been 233 

deposited in Data InDoRES 49  (https://doi.org/10.48579/PRO/LYB70N ) as a public, 234 

community -recognized repository. It is a spreadsheet organised into  five  tabs that document the 235 

database structure, metadata, and traceability.  The  first tab  provides an overview of the database 236 

(e.g., number of species, variables, etc.) and describes the content of each tab in the file  (labelled 237 

‘Read.me’). The second  lists experts and contributors which are involved in data compilation, 238 

together with their taxonomic expertise (labelled ‘Authors_Tax -Repartition’).  The  third  tab 239 

(‘Variables’) provides variable names, definitions, and categories. The  AnthropInsect  dataset is 240 

stored in the ‘Database’ tab, while an additional tab (‘References’) contains the complete list 241 

of reference IDs corresponding to the sources used for trait extraction. Because  AnthropInsect 242 

can be expanded collaboratively, we provide a ‘Completion Guidelines’ document that 243 

describes recommended procedures and quality -control steps to ensure consistent future 244 

database updates. 245

We implemented a series of checks to ensure the reliability and interoperability of 251 

AnthropInsects . Trait definitions and coding followed published recommendations for 252 

standardised trait reporting and harmonisation (e.g. ,31,63 ). To maintain up -to -date taxonomic 253 

classifications and facilitate interoperability with external biodiversity resources, species names 254 

were validated against the GBIF taxonomic backbone, and we recorded the corresponding 255 

GBIF identifiers (e.g., accepted name usage IDs where available 31 ). We also cross -referenced 256 

and integrated information from existing trait databases (e.g. ,58 –60 ) to improve completeness 257 

and consistency. 258 

To support traceability, we associate each trait value with a source and provide a complete list 259 

of reference identifiers used for data extraction. Contributor and expert information is 260 

documented to facilitate follow -up in case of questions or potential errors. Finally, all 261 

automated data retrieval and completion steps (e.g., occurrence downloads, climate extraction, 262 

and scripted field completion) are documented and implemented through version -controlled R 263 

scripts in the repository, ensuring that key processing steps are transparent and reproducible 264 

(https://github.com/Elena -Manfrini/AnthropInsect ). 265
